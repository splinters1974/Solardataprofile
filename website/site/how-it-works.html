How it works

What the analyser does with your data

Plain-English notes on every step, from reading the file to the figures in the report. The full method is in the analyser itself: every finding has a "show calculation and evidence" link.

The analyser reads every half-hourly reading, checks it, works out what the site uses when it's open and when it's shut, and prices the difference at your rates. It shows where to look, not what to fix.

1. Reading your files

Load one file per meter, as Excel (.xlsx, .xls) or CSV. Three layouts work:

  • Day per row: a date, then 48 half-hourly readings (the most common supplier format).
  • Day per column: the same, turned on its side.
  • Timestamped: one reading per row with its date and time.

The analyser tells you how it read each file: which column held the dates, whether readings were kWh or kW, and anything it ignored. Files covering more than a year are analysed over the last 12 months by default; you can pick any other period, and long-term trends still use the whole file.

2. Checking the data

Before showing any figure, the analyser looks for problems that would make it wrong:

  • missing readings and gaps (left out, never counted as zero)
  • days of zero readings, which usually mean a meter or data fault
  • clock-change days and duplicate rows, which it holds back for you to decide
  • single readings far above the normal peak

Each site gets a verdict (Good, Check or Poor) and you can exclude anything you know is wrong, with a reason that's kept with the analysis.

3. Opening hours

Out-of-hours findings depend on knowing when a building should be open. Every site starts with typical office hours, clearly marked as unconfirmed. Set the hours for all sites at once or one by one, including closures, bank holidays and overnight shifts. Findings that rely on unconfirmed hours carry lower confidence.

4. Base load and out-of-hours use

The base load is the level the site rarely drops below: the 5th percentile of all valid half-hourly demand. It's what never switches off, such as servers, fridges and standby loads.

Closed-hours excess is energy used while the site is shut, above that base load. This is the figure the analyser ranks sites by, because it is often the cheapest consumption to tackle: through controls, time clocks and building management settings rather than new equipment.

5. Costs and annual figures

Costs are energy cost at the rates you enter: one flat rate, or time bands such as day and night. They exclude standing charges, capacity charges and other fixed costs. They show what a pattern costs now, not a guaranteed saving.

Annual figures are scaled from the valid readings. With less than about 11 months of good data they are marked indicative, and under six months they aren't shown at all.

6. Findings

Each finding has two separate labels. Impact is how much it matters, based on cost and share of use. Confidence is how good the evidence is, based on data quality and whether the hours are confirmed. A high-impact, low-confidence finding is worth checking, not quoting.

7. Capacity and headroom

Enter your agreed supply capacity (in kVA, from your bill or connection agreement) and an assumed power factor. The analyser compares every half hour against it and works out how much constant new load the site could add before it runs short. It's a screen, not confirmation from your network operator.

What it can't tell you

  • Whole-site data shows when demand happens, not which equipment causes it. Findings are things to check on site.
  • Figures aren't adjusted for weather or changes in occupancy.
  • Carbon figures use the factor you enter, one value for the year.
